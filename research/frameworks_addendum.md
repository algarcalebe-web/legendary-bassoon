# Framework addendum: what the five uploaded Deneffe decks add to the summary (2024 course decks)

The source decks are Strategy – Approach; The Strategy Process (abridged); Internal: Core Competence Analysis; Strategic Position Analysis; Markets with Network Effects. Only the frameworks are kept here. Course case examples are deliberately left out, per the brief.

| Framework element | What the decks add beyond `Deneffe_Frameworks_Summary.md` | How we apply it |
|---|---|---|
| **Profit measure** | Success is judged by **economic profit**: EVA = NOPAT − WACC × invested capital = (ROIC − WACC) × IC. It is not accounting profit. | Each option is judged on its WTP–cost wedge **and** the capital it ties up. Employing staff adds working capital (payroll before client payment, a relief bench). A capital-light design raises ROIC. |
| **Three analyses** | Strategy rests on external attractiveness, internal capabilities and **barriers to imitation** (rivals' ability and willingness to copy). | Imitation is analysed per rival, not just listed. |
| **"Job to be done" and monetization** | External analysis identifies the commercial potential of meeting a need ("the job to be done"), using the right monetization mechanism. | Each need is stated as a job (for example, "keep the household running across three countries without legal exposure"). The value metric is chosen to match it. |
| **Positioning** | Generic strategies (differentiation, cost leadership, "stuck in the middle") prove nothing. A position pays off depending on the distribution of preferences, the cost of serving them, rivals' positions, and the ability and willingness to imitate. Any position should be delivered at the lowest cost, and any cost position should carry maximum WTP. | The conclusion is the wedge, never a label. |
| **Grand Strategy outcomes** | Hypotheses on the most attractive options; an **ambition level**; **a priori boundaries** (businesses we will and will not consider); captured ideas. Vision, mission and values are statements of intent, not facts. | We state the ambition and the boundaries (for example: no owned aircraft, homes or inventory). Per the brief, no mission or vision statement. |
| **Limits of Grand Strategy** | A subjective basis; high/medium/low gut scores; little customer input; revenue forecasts on ad hoc assumptions rather than customer choice evidence; excessive belief in the vision; resistance to change. | Every forecast is labelled an assumption. Operational Strategy (trade-off interviews, choice evidence) validates, adapts or rejects. |
| **Core competence analysis: status** | Four statuses: I expired, II existing (current business), III under development (potential), IV needed in future (scenario-based). Classes: base, key, pacing. | Each competence gets class, position and status. |
| **Core competence: diagnostics** | Being a clear leader in a **base** competence is wasted investment. Less than favourable in a base competence is a fatal gap. Check for overinvestment in competences customers don't value. Base positions on evidence against rivals, not self-rating. | Base gaps are flagged as must-fix (partner or buy). Key and pacing competences are where differentiation must come from. |
| **SPA: criteria** | Tailor the criteria to the market. Attractiveness covers size, growth, competitive intensity, WTP, ease of doing business, risk and complexity. Position measures the ability to meet needs, or meet them at lower cost, than rivals. The more similar the segments, the more specific the criteria. | Scored sub-criteria per segment, not gut placement. |
| **SPA: display** | One slide. Bubble size = turnover or expected revenue. **Non-circular shapes for options not yet launched.** Medium-term horizon. Use in Grand Strategy, then firm up the numbers in a Phase 2 (operational) study. | New options are drawn as non-circular shapes, with bubbles sized on labelled expected revenue. |
| **Networks: types** | One-, two- or three-sided networks; same-side vs cross-side effects; positive or negative (congestion; same-side competition among sellers). | The staff pool is two-sided (families and staff): cross-side positive, same-side negative among families competing for scarce staff at peak. |
| **Networks: implications** | The objective is many high-value interactions, triggered by **marquee users**, before moving to adjacent geographies, segments or markets. Pricing below marginal cost, or permanent subsidy of one side, can be rational. Traditional barriers may not hold. **Envelopment** by a platform with an overlapping user base that bundles the function. | Win marquee family offices first. Consider subsidising the staff side (training, insurance, benefits). Watch envelopment by agencies or EOR platforms. |
| **Winner-take-all** | All three conditions must hold. Condition 1 (high multi-homing cost for at least one side) is sufficient but not necessary. | Applied unchanged. |

## Pricing and Operational Strategy decks

Source decks: Objectives and course positioning; Pricing methods and monetization; Pricing and discounting of value-added services; Pricing and strategy: WTP, demand and market entry; SaaS case questions. The 2023 and 2024 versions of each deck are near-identical. Case examples are left out.

| Framework element | What the decks add | How we apply it |
|---|---|---|
| **Why pricing matters, correctly** | The "1% price = +11% profit" rule ignores elasticity. Every demand curve has an inelastic part (a price rise lifts revenue) and an elastic part (a price rise cuts it). Judge where the current price sits. | No blanket "premium" claims. Price is set from evidence on switching points. |
| **Cost-plus fails** | Lower-than-expected volume raises unit cost, which raises price, which cuts volume further: a death spiral. Only marginal cost against marginal revenue sets price. Fixed costs do not change the profit-maximising price. | Price staff services on value against the next best alternative, never on cost plus margin. |
| **Value map, worked** | Ceiling = price of the next best alternative + quantified benefits (cost savings, revenue gains, reduced hassle) − quantified drawbacks. Walk-away price = our marginal cost (including our cost of capital where we carry working capital or stock). The value price sits inside the wedge and shares it. | Value map with CALC examples. The floor includes the cost of capital on payroll advanced before client payment. |
| **WTP estimation methods** | (1) Econometrics/ML: needs historic data, so it doesn't work for a new offer. (2) Measuring benefits (value mapping): works when benefits are measurable. (3) Indirect interviews: conjoint/discrete choice, or the Trade-Off Method. Direct methods are a no-go: Van Westendorp, open-ended, monadic, Gabor-Granger, key-purchasing-criteria ratings. Stated purchase intent over-predicts actual purchase. | Measurable benefits (agency fees avoided, penalties avoided) → value map. Intangibles (trust, discretion, continuity) → TOM. |
| **Trade-Off Method protocol** | Start with easy preference questions. Avoid anchoring: let the respondent state X. Find the switching point against the best alternative, then iterate from the elicited value to catch non-linearity. Run face to face or assisted online. Five steps: plan interviews, design the questionnaire, pre-test and redesign, interview interactively, process with decision analysis. Rests on completeness, transitivity and additivity of preferences. | The interview guide follows this protocol: an easy opener, unanchored X, iteration, a best-alternative baseline. |
| **TOM outputs** | Customer-choice-based demand forecasts for any offering; segments based on measured WTP differences; the optimal offering, launch price and USP. | The validation plan says what each interview round must deliver. |
| **Market-entry approach (Operational Strategy)** | Total market = everyone who would buy if all bought from us. Potential = those who consider buying or switching within X months. Accessible = those whose must-haves we already meet. Interview subjects come from the potential and accessible market. Six questions: market size; entry barriers and how to overcome them; differentiating features that overcome switching costs; target subsegments; financials to a positive EBIT year; short-term actions. If organic growth can't hit targets, look at partnerships or M&A. | Funnel definitions are tightened. Switching value against the incumbent agency or in-house staff is measured, not assumed. |
| **Transparent vs non-transparent markets** | In transparent markets a price cut to one customer spills over to others. In non-transparent markets, first-degree discrimination is possible. Caveats: high-WTP buyers disguise themselves; arbitrage through intermediaries; negotiated "flexible pricing" ends up rewarding negotiating skill and invites sales-driven discounts; perceived unfairness raises price sensitivity. Negotiation suits complex, infrequent, non-comparable purchases. | UHNW staffing is opaque and infrequent, so negotiated retainers are defensible. Use rules to stop discount creep. |
| **Third-degree price discrimination and fences** | Different prices for segments. Fence with small product changes, by removing a must-have for the high-WTP segment, or by adding a feature that is unacceptable to it. | Fence with service-level differences (response time, relief guarantee, jurisdiction coverage), not price alone. |
| **Two-part pricing, mechanics** | Per-use fee = marginal cost; fixed fee = consumer surplus. When WTP per use falls with use, flat fees beat per-use fees. | Membership or retainer (captures surplus) plus staff-days at marginal cost (salary, insurance, compliance). |
| **Bundling, mechanics** | Bundle only when relative WTP for the items reverses across segments. Otherwise unbundle. Design dimensions: bundle or unbundle; hardware/product vs service; fixed vs variable fee; whom to charge; with whom to share revenue; whether to signal intentions publicly. | Test whether families value staff supply and compliance in reverse order to family offices. Bundle only if they do. |
| **Value metric (SaaS case questions)** | Charge for the metric most correlated with value delivered. Consider outcome-based fees, whether to offer unlimited usage, and tiered bundles by buyer persona vs unbundled offers. | Candidate metrics: per staff-day, per rotation, per household per year, or outcome-based (for example, fee only when a rotation completes lawfully). |
| **Value-added services: price waterfall risk** | A price for every add-on can give an unrealistic total against rivals in competitive markets. Map the standard offering, extra services **to** the customer and extra services **from** the customer. Quantify the marginal cost or saving of each. Final structure: base price, then premiums (minimum price for extra service levels), then discounts (maximum discount for services the customer gives us, kept below the cost they save). | Standard offering: placement plus compliance. Premiums: rush relief, extra jurisdictions. Discounts: long notice, flexible dates, multi-year commitment, referrals. |
| **VAS decision checklist** | Does the feature carry a sizeable variable cost? Is it used often? Do rivals offer it, and do they charge? Are we a recognised price leader? If rivals give it free: give it free and, when use is frequent and costly, discount customers who cut use. The discount must stay below marginal cost. | Applied per service in the pricing slides. |
| **Fad-Free Strategy scope** | Operational Strategy covers pricing new products and services, defending against entrants, entering new markets, pricing services to and from the customer, and markets where customers care only about price. | Validation plan and entry questions. |
