## 1. Purpose, process and the roles of SCQ and the pyramid

| Element | What it says (precise) [slide] | (a) Strategy research deck | (b) UHNW family mobility office analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| The consulting challenge | You often have to solve a complex issue against a tight deadline, through a clear report delivered persuasively. The four corners are complex issue, tight deadline, clear report and persuasive. [s.3, 19] | Fit the scope of research to the deadline and to the decision, not to curiosity. Build the deck so a reader with little time gets the answer quickly. | Frame the venture as one complex issue (staffing operations, multi-jurisdiction employment and immigration law, UHNW selling) that needs a go/no-go by a fixed date. It is not an open-ended landscape study. |
| Four-step cycle | (1) **Analyze the issue**: find the real, often complex issue and break it into a set of discrete, manageable questions. (2) **Plan your work**: assign responsibilities and deadlines for the tasks that gather the answers in time. (3) **Structure your findings**: build a convincing story that is clear, evidence-based, logical, comprehensive and conclusive. (4) **Deliver the message**: persuade the audience to take the actions that flow from your conclusions. [s.4, 19] | Use it as the production workflow: tree, then workplan, then storyline, then final deck. The deck must end in actions, not observations. | Run the work in the same order: key-question tree; research plan (family-office heads, estate managers, immigration and employment counsel, staff); storyline; go/no-go plus first actions (pilot corridor, partners). |
| Pyramid Principle as a general instrument | The pyramid is used through the whole assignment. It breaks a complex question into manageable pieces (issue analysis). It lowers the risk of faulty or incomplete logic. It is the basis for an efficient project plan that can be adapted over time. It helps you communicate with team members. It builds a logical story from individual arguments that are easy to understand. It lets the audience spend its limited mental energy on your conclusion rather than on understanding your text. [s.6] | Keep one master tree as research plan, team brief and storyline at once. Update it as hypotheses change; do not keep a separate outline. | The same tree serves as the diligence plan you show partners and investors and as the skeleton of the pitch. |
| SCQ at the start of an assignment | Makes you think through the real issue that bothers the client. Shows critical gaps in your understanding of the background. Lets you test your understanding of the issue with the client, and may help the client think more clearly too. [s.5] | Write the SCQ before any research and get the sponsor to sign it off. Any background you cannot write as S is a research gap. | Test the draft SCQ with a few target families or family-office COOs. Find out whether the real pain is staff logistics or legal liability for compliance, because the answer changes the key question. |
| SCQ when reporting | In the introduction it reminds the audience of what triggered the report. By focusing on the key issue it secures the audience's willingness to spend time on it. It makes sure you and the audience start "on the same page". [s.5] | Open the deck with the SCQ. | The investor/partner deck opens with the SCQ before any market sizing. |
| How SCQ and the pyramid divide the work | Part 1, Introduction: remind the reader of the (implicit) key question in her mind that your text will answer. The SCQ is the tool for a catchy introduction. Part 2, Argumentation: give the essential, distinct arguments that together lead logically to the answer to the one key question. The pyramid is the tool for thinking and writing efficiently and effectively. In short, the SCQ produces the key question and the pyramid supports writing the argument. [s.8] | Introduction chapter = SCQ only. Everything after it is argument that answers the Q. | Same split: the context slides end on the Q, and every later slide answers part of it. |
| SCQ components (as used) | S = established, uncontroversial background (position, track record). C = the change that threatens it or forces a choice. Q = the single decision question that follows; it becomes the KEY QUESTION at the top of the issue tree. [s.9, 14] | S holds only facts the audience already accepts. C names the trigger (see "Triggers" in section 4). Q is one decision question. | My own illustration. **S:** UHNW families run several residences in several countries and depend on trusted household staff. **C:** staff increasingly have to move with the family, each cross-border move creates immigration, employment, social-security and tax exposure, and agencies and law firms handle these separately. **Q:** Can a combined travelling-staff-pool and compliance service win enough families, at a margin that covers its fixed compliance infrastructure? |

## 2. Issue analysis (issue tree): construction rules

| Element | What it says (precise) [slide] | (a) Strategy research deck | (b) UHNW family mobility office analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | Issue analysis breaks the client's key question into manageable pieces that are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. [s.9] | Every research workstream must hang off a node of the tree. | Every diligence workstream (demand, regulation, staff supply, economics, channels) must hang off a node of the tree. |
| Guideline 1: use an existing framework | Where possible, use an existing framework for this kind of issue. [s.10, 18] | Before inventing a tree, check whether an established framework (industry analysis, market entry, launch strategy) already covers the question. | A market-entry or launch-strategy framework fits (see the template rows below). Do not hand-build from scratch. |
| Guideline 2: yes/no key issue at the top | If no framework exists, build the tree with the key issue at the top, phrased as yes/no. [s.10] | Top node, for example: "Should we invest in X?" | "Should we launch a family mobility office in corridor X, and can it reach positive EBIT by year N?" |
| Guideline 3: brainstorm the heading questions | Under the key issue, brainstorm the sub-questions that must be answered to address the top question. Use external experts and your team for input. [s.10] | Run a team/expert brainstorm before fixing the level-2 questions. | Bring in an immigration lawyer, a payroll/employer-of-record specialist, an estate manager and a family-office head to set the level-2 questions. |
| Guideline 4: depth | For each heading question, add sub-questions at the next level. Try to limit the tree to at most 4 levels. [s.10] | A tree deeper than 4 levels means the key question is too broad or the branches are badly cut. | Keep to: key question, heading questions (e.g. demand, legality, advantage, economics), sub-questions, measurable checks. |
| Guideline 5: closed yes/no form | All questions are in closed yes/no form. [s.10] | Rewrite "What is the market size?" as "Is the market larger than threshold T?" so that each answer is decisive. | "Are there more than N households with residences in 2+ countries and 5+ staff in the target corridor?" |
| MECE | The set of questions must be mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (complete). [s.11, 17] | Siblings must not overlap; together they must fully answer their parent. | Do not split "regulation" and "visas" as siblings, because they overlap. Split by type of obligation instead: right to work / employer obligations / social security / tax residency / data and background checks. |
| Remove redundancy | Remove redundant questions: ones that may be "interesting" but are not essential or relevant to answering the question one level up. [s.11, 17] | Cut any slide or analysis that does not move a parent answer. | Cut, for example, "luxury travel trends" unless it changes the demand answer. |
| Tree anatomy (down to work) | Key issue question, then heading questions, then sub-questions, then **hypotheses**, then **evidence** items, then **tasks**. [s.12, 13] | Each leaf states: hypothesis, the evidence that would confirm or refute it, and the task that produces that evidence. | Leaf example: hypothesis "pooled staff can be billed for at least X% of paid days"; evidence: utilization at comparable agencies plus family demand calendars; task: interviews plus a benchmark model. |
| Task types | Decide which tasks gather every piece of required evidence: desk research, field interviews, internal data analysis, benchmarking, workshop, financial modeling, and so on. [s.12] | Tag each slide's evidence with its task type. | Desk: UHNW census sources and visa rules. Interviews: families, estate managers, lawyers, staff. Benchmarking: agency and EOR fee structures. Workshop: with candidate referral partners. Modeling: unit economics. |
| Project plan | Turn the task list into a project plan: responsibilities, effort and deadlines. Tasks are assigned to named people. [s.12, 13] | Use a workplan table: leaf, task, owner, effort, due date. | Same table; give each corridor's regulatory checks one named owner. |
| Vertical build-up, step 1 (START) | Make sure the one question at the top is the Q from the SCQ, converted to yes/no. [s.16] | Check that the deck's key question matches the intro's Q word for word. | Check that the SCQ Q and the top node are identical. |
| Vertical build-up, step 2 (hypothesis-driven) | Start at the top. Write a hypothetical yes/no answer followed by "because…" arguments. Convert the arguments into yes/no questions. Move to the level below and repeat. [s.16] | Draft the answer on day one and derive the branches from the reasons. | "Yes, because (1) enough multi-jurisdiction families exist, (2) no incumbent takes on staffing and compliance liability together, (3) pooled-staff utilization can pass break-even, (4) private banks and multi-family offices will refer clients." Each "because" becomes a level-2 yes/no question. |
| Vertical build-up, step 3 (STOP rule) | Keep breaking questions down until each one maps to a single information need, a single information source and a single gathering task needed for a clear answer. [s.16] | Stop decomposing when one task can answer the leaf. | "Do domestic staff of nationality A need a separate permit for stays over N days in country B?" has one need, one source (counsel or the official rule) and one task. |
| Vertical build-up, step 4 | The answers at one level must give the answer to the question one level up. [s.16] | Test each chapter: if all its headlines are true, is the chapter's claim proven? | If demand, legality, advantage and economics are all "yes", the go decision must follow. If not, a branch is missing. |
| Horizontal build-up checks | At each level ask: (1) Are the questions mutually exclusive? Collectively exhaustive? (2) Have irrelevant questions been removed? [s.17] | Run this check on each row of chapter titles and on the headlines within each chapter. | Run it on the level-2 set before any research starts. |
| Exception for existing frameworks | If a logical framework already exists, use it instead. The course's example keeps **open "what/which" questions** (not yes/no). Its key question names the success metric, the deadline and the means, and allows "if any" (no-go is a valid answer). It adds a **contingency**: if targets cannot be met by the stated means, other options (partnerships, M&A…) must be investigated. Framework sequence: (1) size of the potential market; (2) entry barriers to overcome, and how; (3) differentiating features that overcome clients' switching costs; (4) client segments to target; (5) what can be achieved financially over the horizon through organic growth; (6) actions to take in the short term. The course cites Deneffe & Vantrappen, *Fad-Free Strategy* (2020), Ch. 3 for this framework. [s.18] | For a launch or growth deck, use this six-part spine as the chapters. Phrase the key question with metric, date, means and "if any". | "What launch strategy, if any, would let the family mobility office reach positive EBIT by year N through organic growth?" Then: market size; barriers (trust, referral access, licensing) and how to overcome them; differentiators that overcome families' switching costs from in-house staff and incumbent agencies; target segments; financials over the horizon; short-term actions. Contingency: if organic growth falls short, assess a partnership with a multi-family office or private bank, or buying an agency. |
| Template: capability-led ("inside-out") growth tree | Key question: "Can we find new profitable growth opportunities quickly?" Heading questions: (1) Can our technology/capability be applied elsewhere (in existing applications of it; in applications that do not use it yet)? (2) Do we have a competitive advantage over existing solutions? (3) Can we credibly convince the providers or users of existing solutions to switch? (4) Can we do so profitably? [s.9] | Use when the deck asks where an existing capability can be redeployed. | Use if the founders bring an existing asset (for example a compliance or staffing practice): can it extend to travelling household staff, does it beat incumbent solutions, will families or their agencies switch, is it profitable? |
| Template: market-entry tree (generic form of the course tree) | **(1) Knock-out:** does the market fail any of our must-have criteria? **(2) Attractive and accessible segments?** Sub-questions: segments of large size today; segments with large growth potential (large potential end-state size; strong or reinforcing demand drivers); segments with low competitive intensity (low rivalry among established players; major entry barriers for a new player; scope to substitute existing offerings; buyer power; supplier power; regulation that limits or distorts fair competition, covering licence to operate and ownership/FDI). **(3) Can we create a competitive advantage in certain segments?** Sub-questions: do current assets/capabilities give an edge (unique assets across products, people, technology, cost/price, channels, networks, other; exploiting weaknesses of current or future players); can we favorably build or acquire missing assets/capabilities (attract and tie partners along the value chain; value-creating acquisitions along the value chain); can we build a business model and defend it against competitors' attacks? **(4) Are there entry options with a return in line with the risks?** [s.15] | Default chapter structure for any "should we enter X" deck: knock-outs first, then attractiveness, then right to win, then entry mode and risk-adjusted return. | **(1)** Can domestic staff legally be employed and moved across each target corridor (right to work, social-security coverage while abroad, agency/employer licensing)? Is the liability insurable? A corridor that fails any of these is dropped. **(2)** Households with residences in 2+ countries and N+ staff (size, demand drivers); rivalry (agencies, multi-family offices, concierge firms, employer-of-record and mobility providers); barriers (trust, discretion, referral access); substitutes (in-house family-office HR plus local hires per residence); buyer power (few, demanding clients); supplier power (scarce top staff can be hired away directly by families); regulation (licences, foreign-ownership limits). **(3)** Assets: vetted staff network, compliance tracking technology, referral networks. Incumbent weakness: agencies place staff but do not carry compliance; law firms advise but do not supply staff. Build or buy employer entities or EOR partners; tie in private banks, multi-family offices and insurers. Defensibility: embedded compliance records, staff loyalty, non-solicit terms. **(4)** Referral-only vs EOR partnership vs own entities vs acquisition, each with return against risk; a single-corridor pilot. |

## 3. Report writing and slide rules

| Element | What it says (precise) [slide] | (a) Strategy research deck | (b) UHNW family mobility office analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slide as scarce land | "Writing a slide is like building on a precious plot of land: use its scarce surface area thoughtfully." [s.20] | Cut decoration, repeated content and unsupported graphics. | The same applies, and UHNW-facing material should look restrained anyway. |
| Slide anatomy | Top left: **chapter** label. Top right: optional **signposts** (1. 2. 3.). **Headline**: sums up the slide's message in one sentence. **Body**: the arguments that support the headline; only directly relevant arguments; a mix of text and relevant graphics as appropriate; optional signposts so the reader sees where the page fits in the whole document. Bottom: **sources** (left) and **page number** (right). [s.20] | Every slide gets a one-sentence message headline, a body that only proves it, a source line, a page number, a chapter label and a signpost tracker. | Example headline: "Two of five corridors fail the legal knock-out, so launch in the remaining three." The body holds only the corridor-by-corridor rule evidence, with sources. |
| Pyramid-to-document mapping | Your SCQ is the introduction chapter. The following chapters normally match the first level of questions/answers below the key question, "and so forth". Headlines within a chapter normally match the second level. Evidence for the answers to the lowest pyramid questions goes in the body of the slide. [s.21] | Chapters = level-1 answers; slide headlines = level-2 answers; charts and tables = leaf evidence. | Chapters: demand / legality / right to win / economics and entry mode. Headlines: the answered sub-questions. Bodies: data. |
| Don't copy the whole pyramid | You do not have to reproduce the entire pyramid. Leave out arguments that, with hindsight, matter less. [s.22] | Drop branches that turned out immaterial; keep them in the appendix or remove them. | Drop, for example, a corridor branch that proved irrelevant to the decision. |
| Collapse layers | You may collapse two or more vertical layers into one to avoid an over-layered structure (sub-sub-sub-chapters). [s.22] | At most chapter plus headline in the main deck. | Merge "regulation" and "right to work" layers if that keeps it to two levels. |
| Conclusion upfront (default) | Normally put the conclusion upfront, but depending on audience and circumstances you may deviate from that rule. [s.22] | Answer first. Go inductive only on purpose (for example with a sceptical audience). | Put go/no-go first for investors. With a skeptical family office, you might lead with the compliance risk before the answer. |
| Headline list page | For large documents you can include a page listing all the headlines of a chapter. [s.22] | Add a chapter-opener page that lists that chapter's headlines. | Same, for each long chapter. |
| Executive summary | The top levels of the pyramid can form the basis of an executive summary at the start of the document. [s.22] | Exec summary = key answer plus level-1 answers, possibly with level-2 points. | One page: the go/no-go answer plus four "because" answers. |
| Pragmatism over rigidity | Do not be dogmatic about the report-writing "rules": they are only a means to communicate persuasively. Always use your best judgment to write slides that suit the situation. The method itself (yes/no tree vs graphical decomposition) should also be applied pragmatically. [s.23, 29] | Break a rule only for a stated reason. | Example: a diagram of a family's travel calendar may persuade better than text. |
| Practice shown in the source's own slides (not stated as rules) | Headlines are full-sentence messages and often quantify impact, scope and timing (lever, then amount, then from when, then where). Analyses are labelled "illustrative" and "disguised data". Historic (H) and forecast (F) points are marked. Definitions are footnoted (for example the ratio definition, pre-tax vs after-tax). Every chart has a source line. [s.30-46] | Use the headline form "lever, impact, timing, scope". Mark estimates vs actuals and footnote every metric definition. | Example: "Charging retainers upfront cuts receivables by X days from launch." Mark modeled vs observed figures. |

## 4. Strategy diagnosis and quick-wins method

| Element | What it says (precise) [slide] | (a) Strategy research deck | (b) UHNW family mobility office analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| How strategy arises | Executives think about threats to profitability, which map conceptually onto the "five or six forces". They look for opportunities to counter those threats, or simply spot opportunities in daily contact with customers, employees, partners, suppliers and universities. They "look forward and reason back": anticipate future customer needs and competitors' moves, then work out what that implies today (Strategy Rule #1, citing MIT Sloan Management Review, 2016). Some do this better than others, which may separate winners from losers. Sometimes the CEO runs an explicit collective exercise (off-sites, workshops). [s.25] | Include a "look forward, reason back" section: future needs and competitor moves, then what they mean today. Arrange threats by force. | Look forward: tighter enforcement on cross-border domestic work and social security, and multi-family offices, employer-of-record platforms or concierge firms bundling staff. Reason back: build the compliance engine and referral ties now as the moat. |
| Triggers for an explicit strategy exercise | **Performance factors:** a shock or actual underperformance of the current strategy; expected future underperformance from outside threats (for example commoditization); limits to growth in current markets; a sense of missing major opportunities that get overlooked in daily work or in budgeting, which mostly extrapolates the business as it is. **Internal factors:** a need for alignment on goals (for example with a new CEO); open questions that have circulated for a long time; a sense that management attention should move back from internal matters (for example post-merger integration) to strategy. These exercises often come exactly when the team wants to focus on daily operations because of the underperformance. [s.26] | The Complication in the SCQ should name which trigger applies. | For the venture, the trigger is a missed opportunity (fragmented incumbents) plus outside threats to the status quo for families (compliance exposure). |
| Why diagnose first | Explicit reviews often happen when the company underperforms or expects to. Their payoffs are uncertain while they need considerable people and external resources. Much time (often over a year) can pass between the review and execution, and so before any (uncertain) improvement. So it often makes sense to run a "health check" of the current strategy and get rapid improvement through quick wins. [s.27] | Open long strategy projects with a diagnosis and quick-wins chapter. | Run a 90-day pilot or health check on early corridors before the full build-out. |
| Benefits of diagnosis | Financial results arrive quickly. Top management buys into the idea that performance can improve, and will improve through the review process too. More belief that investing in a strategy review is effective. [s.27] | Show quick wins early in the deck to earn buy-in for the long-term recommendations. | Early proof points (first families served compliantly) earn partner and investor buy-in. |
| Qualitative vs quantitative diagnosis | **Qualitative:** compare performance with best-in-class. It is often hard without specialized external consultants because comparisons are missing, and it is the least important for reaching the objectives. **Quantitative:** use hard measures of how economic profit has evolved, using ROCE over the last 3-5 years; use an issue tree to find the underperforming branches behind the decline; produce quick wins to fix them, wherever possible using benchmarks and common sense. [s.28] | Lead with quantitative decomposition; use qualitative best-in-class only to support it. | Build the venture's return-on-capital tree before launch and use it as the KPI tree after launch. |
| Diagnosis as an issue tree | The goal is the root causes of the deteriorating performance. Option 1: a strict yes/no tree. Top question: "Can we identify the core causes of the deteriorating performance that can be turned around in the short term?" First sub-questions: "Is EBIT/Sales deteriorating and can it be turned around quickly?" and "Is Sales/CE deteriorating and can it be turned around quickly?" Option 2: decompose graphically. Be pragmatic about how best to convey the message. [s.29] | Either form works; pick the one the audience reads fastest. | Pre-launch version: "Can the model earn above its cost of capital by year N?" Sub-questions: "Is EBIT/Sales above X%?" and "Is Sales/CE above Y?" |
| Step 1 | Plot ROCE over 3-5 years (historic vs forecast) against WACC (the spread). [s.30] | Show the trend of return vs cost of capital before any cost detail. | Model ROCE by year against a venture-appropriate WACC. |
| Step 2 | Is the weaker result due to a weaker operating margin (EBIT/Sales) or slower capital turnover (Sales/CE)? Break down cost lines as % of sales and working capital and fixed assets as % of sales. Benchmark net working capital against similar competitors at BU level. Isolate which unit drove a setback. [s.31-32] | Run the two-branch split first, then drill only into the branch that moved. | Margin is driven by bill rate vs loaded staff cost and paid bench time. Turnover is driven by receivables, pre-funded payroll and licence deposits/bonds. |
| ROCE tree | ROCE = EBIT/Sales (operating margin) × Sales/CE (capital turnover). EBIT/Sales splits into COGS/Sales, Depreciation/Sales and SG&A/Sales. Sales/CE splits into Sales/PPE (fixed-asset turnover), Sales/Inventories (inventory turnover), Sales/Accounts receivable, and turnover of other working-capital items. [s.33] | Standard value-driver tree to anchor any performance deck. | Service version: staff cost/sales, travel and permits/sales, compliance team/sales, client acquisition and referral fees/sales; sales/receivables, sales/pre-funded payroll, sales/deposits, sales/technology capex. |
| Step 3 | Assess whether the cost ratios can reasonably come down. Dig into their components. For each subcomponent, check whether the ratio rose and why. Use benchmarks plus targeted interviews ("not rocket science") to recommend targets that are ambitious yet feasible. Avoid "analysis paralysis", because the goal is quick wins that earn buy-in. [s.34] | Set targets by benchmark plus interview, and cap the depth of analysis. | Benchmark each cost line against agency and employer-of-record fee structures plus interviews. Do not over-model before the pilot. |
| Quick-win analysis types | Channel commission as % of net price, by region, vs a target % (giving savings). Discretionary spend (for example customer entertainment) normalized by contribution margin across units, to set target budgets from internal benchmarks. Line-by-line review of promotional spend: keep only spend that yields market intelligence and cut the rest. Facility cost by region: close remote offices and move salespeople to home offices. Price realization: badly negotiated contracts justify price increases, and supplying from a closer plant lifts CM. Sales productivity = net sales per direct sales FTE by country, with target = cross-country average (excluding outliers) + 20%, adjusted for country-specific dynamics. Move FTE time from admin to direct selling. [s.37-45] | Each one is a reusable benchmark slide pattern: a ratio by unit, a target line, and the gap valued in money. | Referral fees as % of fee by channel. Client-event spend per unit of CM. Keep only marketing that brings referrals or intelligence. No physical office per corridor. Review retainer pricing. Deploy staff from the nearest pool base to cut travel cost. Revenue per client-relationship manager with an average + 20% target. |
| Lever families | **Product/customer rationalization:** drop product/customer combinations with CM < 0; in short markets, shift volume to customers who pay more and/or cost less to serve; in long markets, raise volumes or prices at selected customers. **Promotional cost rationalization:** focus spend on gaining market knowledge and cut all ineffective spending. **Sales channel rationalization:** cut agent commissions; let large agents enter orders directly in the company's ERP. **Sales force and office rationalization:** home offices; maximize time spent on direct selling; cut FTEs in underperforming areas. [s.46] | Use these as the MECE set of commercial levers in a margin-improvement chapter. | Drop family/corridor/service combinations with CM < 0 (for example one-off short assignments with heavy permit costs). When staff capacity is scarce, give it to the highest-paying and cheapest-to-serve families. Give large referral partners direct booking. |

## 5. Economics and finance fundamentals in the source, with corrections

| Element | What it says (precise) [slide] | (a) Strategy research deck | (b) UHNW family mobility office analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| ROCE | ROCE = EBIT / Capital Employed. It is similar to ROIC, but usually uses EBIT over CE rather than NOPAT. Many definitions exist. [s.28] | Footnote the exact definition on every return chart. | Capital-light model: ROCE can be high even at thin margins if receivables and pre-funding are kept small. |
| Economic profit and spread | Use hard measures of economic profit, shown as ROCE against WACC (the "WACC spread"). [s.28, 30] | A business creates value only when its return is above its cost of capital. Show the spread, not just the margin. | The test is whether the return on capital employed beats a risk-appropriate WACC once corridors are up and running. |
| Tax-basis consistency | The source flags "(!)" that its ROCE is pre-tax while its WACC is after-tax. [s.30] | Correction: compare like with like (after-tax ROCE/ROIC with after-tax WACC, or pre-tax with pre-tax). Otherwise the spread is overstated. | Apply the same rule in the venture model. |
| Capital employed composition | CE drivers in the tree: PPE (fixed assets), inventories, receivables, other working capital. [s.33] | Note: CE ≈ fixed assets + net working capital. Payables reduce it. | Main capital items are receivables, pre-funded payroll and regulatory deposits/bonds. Retainers paid upfront reduce CE. |
| Receivables label | The source labels Sales/Accounts receivable "(Creditor Turnover)". [s.33] | Correction: receivables turnover is **debtor** turnover. Creditor turnover relates to payables. | Track debtor days on family invoices. |
| Contribution margin | CM = net sales − variable costs (transport, raw materials, agent commissions, variable manufacturing cost…). [s.46] | Rank products/customers by CM. Drop combinations with CM < 0. | Variable costs per deployment: staff wages and employer charges, travel/per-diem, permit fees, insurance, referral fees. Fixed costs: compliance team, technology, entity upkeep, licences. Bench (unbilled) staff time is a fixed cost when staff are salaried. |
| Normalizing ratios | Express cost lines as % of sales and discretionary spend as % of CM to compare units. [s.32, 39] | Compare units only on normalized ratios. | Use per-family and per-corridor ratios. |
| Short vs long markets | Levers differ in "short" vs "long" markets; the source does not define the terms. [s.46] | Interpretation (not in source): short = demand exceeds capacity, so raise prices and allocate capacity; long = excess capacity, so add volume. | Elite staff capacity is likely "short", which favors price and allocation over volume. |

## Storyline checklist (as the source teaches it)

1. **SCQ written first and tested:** Situation (accepted background), Complication (the trigger), Question (one decision question). It was tested with the client/sponsor before the analysis. [s.5, 14]
2. **SCQ = introduction chapter.** It reminds the audience of the trigger and of the key question in their mind, and puts everyone "on the same page". [s.5, 8, 21]

## Econ checks to run on a business case

1. **Single objective.** Is there one objective function? Are the choice variables (scope) and constraints explicit and agreed? Reject "maximise revenue and profit".
2. **Cost tagging.** Is every cost line tagged as marginal, avoidable fixed, unavoidable fixed, sunk or opportunity cost? Does any average or fully allocated figure drive a decision?
3. **Sunk costs.** Are sunk costs excluded from every forward decision (keep, drop, exit, continue)? Is anyone arguing "we've already invested"?
4. **Fixed-cost invariance.** Does the price or volume recommendation change when fixed costs change? If so, the model is wrong (entry and exit decisions excepted).
5. **MR = MC.** Is price or quantity set where MR = MC, or P = MC in price-taking segments? Is anything priced cost-plus on average cost?
6. **MR vs P.** Is MR treated as P where demand slopes down? Is the revenue lost on existing units counted?
7. **Discount spillover.** How transparent is the market? Walk-away discount = (P − MC)/(N+1) when buyers talk; horizon effects make it tighter still.
8. **Revenue vs profit.** Are any KPIs, targets or bonuses revenue-based? Are incentives aligned with contribution?
9. **Portfolio on contribution.** Are keep or drop decisions made on revenue − avoidable cost, with complementarities counted? Is there a fully-allocated death spiral?
10. **Equal margins.** Are scarce resources (staff, capacity, budget) allocated so that marginal returns are equal, not prices or shares? Is opportunity cost in MC wherever capacity binds?
11. **Customer's opportunity cost.** What does the client's next-best alternative cost? That caps the price.
12. **Economic profit.** Are the cost of capital, market pay for founders, working capital and risk or liability tails included? Is return on capital above its cost?
13. **Competitiveness.** Which segments meet the five perfect-competition conditions? In those, assume price-taking and normal long-run returns.
14. **Industry cost curve.** Who is the marginal price-setter? Where does the business sit on the curve? Is it a low-cost supplier earning the gap above its cost?
15. **Will entry compete away profits?** What are the barriers, who are the likely entrants and how fast could they arrive? What specific cost or differentiation advantage survives entry?
16. **Short run vs long run.** Shutdown test: P < AVC. Exit test: expected P < ATC when commitments come up for renewal. Are they kept separate and timed to renewal dates?
17. **Forward pricing.** Are contract prices set on the expected market price over the term, with supply and demand shifts anticipated, not on today's spot?
18. **Windfall vs structural.** Is current profitability a short-run demand shock at fixed capacity? Normalise before valuation or capacity decisions.
19. **Timing.** Is there a window that requires fast entry? What happens to a wait-and-see plan?
20. **Strategy fit.** If undifferentiated, is the plan cost leadership "to the bone"? If differentiated, what evidence of willingness to pay supports it?
21. **Assumptions.** Are ceteris paribus, competitor non-reaction, horizon and capacity assumptions stated on the slides and stress-tested?
